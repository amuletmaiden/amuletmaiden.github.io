Little Sprite’s Storybook

Once upon a time, a strange and sharp-toothed Lady sat in the boughs of an oak tree, quite alone and quite out of place in the middle of the fen. It gnarled and twisted towards the sky, along the ground; an ancient yearning pushing it in every which direction!

The Lady had nestled herself in a crook of the tree and begun to read; she recited poetry in dulcet tones, of love and grief and hateful violence, and the wicked dross between all three.

The Little Sprite could not help but flit down from his home among the leaves and listen. She read poem after poem after poem, and each word captivated him; and in between she would pause and take notes, and he would grow frightfully bored until she began again.

With each break he grew more frustrated. Playing with the ladybugs and aphids did less and less to soothe his irritation. After an especially long poem, to his outrage, the Lady took whole minutes to herself! He barrelled down to confront this injustice:

“Humans! Joyless! Dreadful! Who must each thing by pen and wit command in full, so ne'er they lend a word its own exhort!”

His voice rang like the crisp of autumn air. He reddened his face and crossed his arms and puffed his cheeks in the most vicious pout he could muster. “Hmph!”

The Lady jumped, and sought the source of the insult, until she settled on the Little Sprite and her eyes softened in delight. Barely more than an inch high, held aloft on beeswings and cuddled up in a coat so furry as to threaten to swallow him. Iridescent hair scattered past his shoulders.

“Oh my! What a darling little fairy. Why, you are the prettiest thing I have ever beheld. Your coat, too–who made that for you?”

His pout faltered in the face of the compliment.

“The coat was made by my own hand, of cast-off wings, the orphans of the secret things a moth becomes, when their time as moth is up; a stitcher’s pride in it I place.”

“As is well-deserved! Such fine craftsmanship must surely have taken ages to perfect. How old are you, little thing?”

He creased his brow and clenched his fists and tried and failed to hold onto his indignity.

“Born I was a week ago, now wise and agéd I’ve become; still do I recall the times when leaves were red, not gold.”

The Lady laughs lightly. “To think I should meet such an elder! And yet so youthful and spry. Tell me, what should I call you?”

“A name one keeps a secret thing–this I know by wisdom of my many days. You, I think, may call me Sprite.”

“Sprite! How lovely. You were fond of my reading, weren’t you? I would love to continue, but the sun is setting and soon it’ll be dark. I promise, little Sprite, I’ll read to you tomorrow.”

And she closed her book and hefted her walking-stick, then turned back and asked:

“Do you fear the dark, Sprite?”

“Never! Darkness but a trifle is; always comes, in time, the dawn.”

“Wise indeed!” And so she leaves.

And the next afternoon the Lady did return to that oak in that fen, and sit herself in the crook of its boughs, and read again. She read plays, of assassins and their daggers and their knives. And all the while the Little Sprite straddled her wrist and listened and only mildly grumbled when she paused.

Soon again, the sun was setting; “Dear Sprite, I’m afraid I must go now.”

And she closed her book, and hefted her walking-stick, then turned back and asked:

“Do you fear knives, Sprite?”

“Hah! A knife is naught except a toy, for to carve and cut the cloth. One must its teeth regard, but fear? Never!”

“Careful indeed!” And so she leaves.

Once more, on the third afternoon, the Lady came again to the Little Sprite’s home, and settled herself in the crook of its boughs, and read to him. He settled on her shoulder, leaned against her neck, and listened to stories of terrible isolation and grief. He didn’t even complain when she took her time between passages.

But again the sun sunk low in the sky. “Precious Sprite, I must take my leave.”

And she closed her book, and hefted her walking-stick, then turned back and asked:

“Do you fear being alone, Sprite?”

“Alone? All my life before have I in isolation lived; how could I fear the one and only thing I’ve known?”

“Steadfast indeed!” And so she leaves.

But the fourth afternoon, as the Lady arrives to the tree again and places herself in the crook of its boughs, she asks a question.

“Little Sprite, so long have I known you, all these many days; more than half a week has come and gone since our first meeting. And yet I still do not know your name. Surely, you could entrust such a steadfast friend with your secret?”

He considers. He hems. He haws. He should not–but indeed, so long has he known her, and in that time he hasn’t seen one ounce of ill-will or malice from her. Surely, he could entrust such a steadfast friend with his secret.

“To you, my friend, an indiscretion I might make; for ne'er in all our time together have you wanted for the power over me it may provide. You alone may call me: Bran.”

“Bran? I see. What a lovely name. Come along, now, there are things I must show you.”

“My friend, adore you as I do, I must not from my home go far; your tales must to me suffice.”

“Bran, poor thing, I did not ask. Come along.”

And so he did, all the way home; and I taught him all the fears to which he had been so naïve. I taught him darknesses that do not break for any dawn, lightless months on lightless months until he forgets all sight. I taught him blades which cut so much more than cloth–toys, true, but only to me, until his wings are so torn through as to be useless. I taught him isolation so much crueler than what he thought he knew, no bees or birds to keep him company, until he forgot the world might ever contain someone besides himself.

And then my work began, secret things of which I will not speak, not even to you, my dear, steadfast friend. And when the work was done, I beheld my opus with a stitcher’s pride, hard won over decades of practice and failure:

You, my sweet little Genevieve. Who fears the dark. Who fears the knives. Who fears being alone. But I will shelter you from all these things, and you will be by my side forever.
